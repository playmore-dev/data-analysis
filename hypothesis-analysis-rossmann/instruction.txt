## 3. Main Research Question

### Primary question

**Do Rossmann stores have significantly different average daily sales on promotional days compared with non-promotional days?**

The analysis should determine:

1. Whether a statistically significant difference exists.
2. The direction and magnitude of the difference.
3. How uncertain the estimated difference is.
4. Whether the observed difference is practically meaningful.
5. Whether other factors could explain some of the observed difference.

The main analysis is observational. Do not automatically interpret an association between promotions and sales as proof of causation.

### Stage 3: Hypothesis Testing

#### 3.1 Define the population and estimand

Define the target population as the relevant open-store daily observations represented by the available training data.

Define:

- μP: Mean daily sales during promotional observations.
- μN: Mean daily sales during non-promotional observations.

The initial comparison is the difference between these group means.

Clearly distinguish the raw difference in means from an adjusted promotion association estimated using a statistical model.

#### 3.2 State the statistical hypotheses

Use the following two-sided hypotheses for the primary test:

H₀: μP − μN = 0

H₁: μP − μN ≠ 0

Use a significance level of α = 0.05.

These hypotheses test whether average daily sales differ between the two observed conditions.

Do not change the hypotheses after inspecting the p-value.

#### 3.3 Select the appropriate statistical test

Begin with **Welch's independent two-sample t-test** as a transparent baseline comparison.

Welch's test does not require equal population variances, but its ordinary independent-observation interpretation does not automatically account for repeated observations from the same store or shared calendar effects.

Therefore, assess the suitability of the baseline test and supplement it with an analysis that better accounts for the data structure.

Consider the following approaches:

**A. Baseline comparison**

- Compare promotional and non-promotional sales using Welch's t-test.
- Report the test statistic, degrees of freedom where available, and p-value.
- Calculate a confidence interval for the mean difference.

**B. Store-level paired comparison**

Where sufficient comparable observations exist, calculate mean sales for promotional and non-promotional conditions within each store.

- Retain stores with observations in both conditions.
- Calculate the within-store difference.
- Apply a paired t-test to the store-level differences if its assumptions are reasonably satisfied.
- Examine the distribution of the paired differences and consider a suitable alternative if assumptions are not defensible.

Explain that the store-level analysis estimates a different, store-weighted comparison from the pooled daily-record analysis.

**C. Adjusted regression analysis**

Where feasible, estimate a model that accounts for relevant sources of dependence and confounding.

For example, consider a model with:

- Sales as the outcome.
- `Promo` as the primary explanatory variable.
- Store fixed effects.
- Day-of-week and calendar controls.
- School holiday and state holiday indicators where appropriate.
- Standard errors clustered by store, with limitations discussed.

Choose a specification justified by the available data. Avoid adding every variable indiscriminately.

If the full dataset is too large for a proposed method, use a justified and reproducible strategy. Do not silently switch to an arbitrary subset.

#### 3.4 Check assumptions and limitations

- Repeated observations within stores.

#### 3.5 Interpret the statistical result

For each primary test:

1. State the null and alternative hypotheses.
2. Report the chosen test and its rationale.
3. Report the test statistic and p-value.
4. Report the estimated difference and confidence interval.
5. Compare the p-value with α = 0.05.
6. State whether the null hypothesis is rejected or whether there is insufficient evidence to reject it.
7. Explain the result in plain English.
8. Distinguish statistical significance from practical significance.

A p-value above 0.05 does not prove that the groups are identical.

A p-value below 0.05 does not establish that the difference is commercially important or caused by the promotion.

## 5. Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Reporting

Use the following measures where appropriate.

| Metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Sample size | Establish the amount of available evidence. |
| Mean and median | Summarize typical sales. |
| Standard deviation and IQR | Describe variability. |
| Absolute mean difference | Quantify the difference in sales units. |
| Percentage difference | Express the difference relative to non-promotional mean sales. |
| Test statistic and p-value | Evaluate evidence against the null hypothesis. |
| 95% confidence interval | Quantify uncertainty around the estimated difference. |
| Cohen's d or Hedges' g | Describe standardized effect size when suitable. |
| Adjusted regression coefficient | Estimate the promotion association conditional on included controls. |
| Cluster-robust confidence interval | Account for within-store dependence when using a suitable clustered regression specification. |

Calculate percentage difference as:

Percentage difference = [(Mean promotional sales − Mean non-promotional sales) / Mean non-promotional sales] × 100

Check that the denominator is meaningful and nonzero.

Use a 95% confidence interval and α = 0.05 unless there is a documented reason to choose otherwise.

Do not use prediction metrics such as RMSE or R² as the primary evaluation criteria for this hypothesis-testing project. They may be reported for a separate predictive model, but they do not replace inferential statistics.
